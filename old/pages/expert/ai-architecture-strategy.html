AI Architecture Strategy

AI architecture should define where AI sits, what it can access, what it can do, and how humans control it.

Expertise: Expert Audience: Architects and platform leaders Use when: Moving from isolated tools to managed AI capabilities Version: 1.0

Who this is for

This guide is for architecture, platform, and technology leaders deciding how AI capabilities fit into systems, data, workflows, and governance.

What you will learn

  • How to treat AI as an architectural capability, not random tool sprawl.
  • Where assistants, coding agents, retrieval, and workflow automation differ.
  • Why auditability and lifecycle management must be designed in early.

Strategic frame

AI capabilities are part of the enterprise architecture when they access data, influence decisions, modify repositories, call tools, or automate workflows. Architecture should define integration patterns, permissions, observability, lifecycle ownership, and human control points before adoption spreads through unmanaged exceptions.

Capability map

General assistants

Support drafting, summarizing, and analysis with data-use boundaries.

Business assistants

Use approved enterprise tools for role-specific workflows and records.

Coding assistants

Assist technical teams under repository, test, and review controls.

Agentic development

Edit files and run commands only inside explicit permission and checkpoint boundaries.

Retrieval systems

Ground answers in approved sources with permission-aware retrieval and citations.

Workflow automation

Act through controlled tools, approval gates, monitoring, and rollback paths.

Good use and risky use

Good use

Design AI capabilities with platform ownership, approved integration patterns, auditable tool access, lifecycle controls, and clear human checkpoints.

Risky use

Let every team connect AI tools to systems independently without identity, permission, logging, vendor, or decommissioning standards.

Leadership questions

1. Which AI capabilities are enterprise platforms and which are local tools? 2. What data, systems, and actions can each AI capability access? 3. Who owns integration standards and lifecycle management? 4. What logs prove what the AI saw, suggested, or did? 5. Where must humans approve, monitor, or stop the workflow?

Governance checklist

Ownership: Assign platform and business owners for each AI capability. Integration: Standardize approved connectors, APIs, identity, and permission patterns. Auditability: Capture prompts, retrieved sources, tool calls, approvals, and outcomes where appropriate. Lifecycle: Define onboarding, review, versioning, vendor changes, and retirement. Controls: Match human control points to the capability's access and action level.

Key takeaway

AI strategy becomes durable when architecture makes access, actions, ownership, and human control visible.
