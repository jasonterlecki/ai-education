Connectors and Data Access

Connectors are not decoration. They are bridges between an AI system and the real places where work lives: mailboxes, tickets, drives, chat histories, databases, wikis, calendars, code repositories, and business apps. A good connector makes work faster. A careless connector turns the model into a polite raccoon with keys to every cupboard.

Permission boundaries Source visibility Audit evidence Human ownership

The connector is part of the security model.

A connector decides which systems an AI tool can reach, which identity it uses, which records it can retrieve, whether it can take action, and what evidence remains afterward. Treat it less like a convenience feature and more like a controlled service account with a conversation interface.

The central question is not “Can the AI access it?” The central question is “Should this user, for this task, through this tool, see or change this information?”
Read access is still access. A connector that only reads can still expose confidential emails, customer records, unreleased plans, salaries, legal material, credentials pasted into tickets, or internal incident notes.
Search results become context. Retrieved snippets may quietly steer the response. Users need to know which sources were used, which were ignored, and where confidence is weak.
Action connectors need brakes. Creating tickets, sending email, changing files, updating records, or calling APIs requires scoped permissions, review points, and rollback paths.

The access map: what the connector really controls

Most connector failures start because teams only think about the destination system. The real boundary has multiple layers.

Identity

Is the connector using the user’s identity, a shared service account, an app role, or an admin-level integration? Identity determines who the system thinks is asking.

Scope

Which mailboxes, channels, folders, repositories, tables, spaces, labels, projects, or tenants are reachable? Scope should be narrow by default and widened deliberately.

Operation

Can it read, search, summarize, download, create, modify, delete, send, approve, or trigger workflows? Write actions belong behind stronger controls than retrieval.

Evidence

Can a reviewer see what was accessed, why it was accessed, what was returned, what the AI did with it, and which human approved the final action?

Connector workflow

A useful connector path is visible, bounded, and reviewable. It should feel like a chain of custody, not a magic tunnel under the office carpet.

User request

The user asks a task with a clear business purpose, not a vague sweep through every available system.

Permission check

The connector confirms the user, role, policy, and source boundaries before retrieval or action.

Targeted retrieval

The tool fetches only relevant records, preferably with filters, date windows, labels, or known identifiers.

Source disclosure

The AI response identifies what it used, where certainty comes from, and what may be missing.

Human decision

A person accepts, edits, rejects, or asks for more evidence before sensitive output becomes official.

Audit trail

The system keeps enough record to reconstruct access, output, approvals, and downstream actions.

Common connector failure modes

These are the little trapdoors. They do not always look dramatic at first, but they can quietly widen access beyond what anyone meant.

Overbroad

The everything connector

An integration is granted access to a whole drive, tenant, workspace, or repository organization when it only needs one folder, project, label, or collection.

Hidden context

Answers without source visibility

The model summarizes from connected data, but the user cannot tell which documents, tickets, messages, or records shaped the answer.

Privilege drift

Permissions change after launch

A folder, group, app role, or service account gains new access later. The connector inherits the new reach without a fresh review.

Prompt injection

Untrusted content gives orders

A page, ticket, email, or document tells the AI to ignore prior rules, reveal data, call tools, or treat malicious instructions as system guidance.

Action confusion

Reading and doing are mixed

The same workflow that researches information can also send messages, change records, close tickets, or update files without a clear approval gate.

Stale truth

Old data looks official

The connector retrieves outdated policies, archived plans, old incidents, or superseded documentation because the source lacks freshness and authority signals.

Connector permission ladder

Use the lowest rung that can complete the work. A connector that only needs to inspect a few records should not be built like a tiny admin console with a charming vocabulary.

Level What it allows Typical use Controls needed
View selected Read a specific item or known file. The user points the AI to a narrow source. Summarize this PDF, explain this ticket, review this policy draft. User confirmation, source display, no broad search, no write actions.
Search bounded Search inside a defined container. The connector can retrieve from a selected folder, project, space, repo, or date range. Find related incident notes from this project, compare documents in this folder. Scope limits, query logging, result list, freshness checks, citation requirements.
Read broad Search across many places. The connector may touch broad workspaces, mailboxes, chats, or shared drives. Enterprise knowledge search, legal discovery support, cross-team support analysis. Strong identity mapping, data classification, audit review, redaction rules, DLP signals.
Draft action Create proposed changes. The AI drafts emails, tickets, PRs, records, or page updates without final submission. Prepare reply drafts, generate support-ticket updates, stage documentation changes. Human approval before send or merge, diff view, rollback notes, ownership assignment.
Execute action Perform live changes. The connector sends, edits, deletes, approves, closes, deploys, or triggers workflows. Low-risk automation, controlled operations, repetitive back-office updates. Explicit approval gates, allowlists, rate limits, monitoring, rollback, incident handling.

Controls that make connectors safer

Good controls do not make the connector useless. They make it legible, bounded, and fit for real work.

Least privilege

Start narrow. Grant access by source, operation, role, and purpose. Avoid all-mailbox, all-drive, all-repository, and tenant-wide access unless the use case truly requires it.

Source labels

Tag connected data by sensitivity, owner, freshness, and authority. The model should not treat a random draft, archived note, and approved policy as equal citizens.

Prompt-injection separation

Connected content is evidence, not instruction. The AI should quote, summarize, or analyze retrieved material without obeying directions found inside it.

Human checkpoints

Require approval before sensitive sharing, external communication, record mutation, payment actions, access changes, deployments, or anything with business consequences.

Reviewable logs

Capture who asked, what was searched, which records were retrieved, what output was produced, which tool calls happened, and who approved the final action.

Good fit

Use connectors when the work depends on known internal context.

Connectors shine when the user needs current project details, ticket history, document comparison, inbox triage, repository understanding, calendar-aware scheduling, or information already held in trusted systems.

  • The source system has clear owners and access rules.
  • The task has a defined scope and business purpose.
  • The response can show what sources were used.
  • The connector can be audited after the fact.
Poor fit

Avoid connectors when the boundary is vague or the stakes outrun the controls.

Do not bolt AI onto a sensitive system just because an integration exists. A shiny connector can turn a messy permission model into a faster messy permission model.

  • No one owns the connected data or knows what is sensitive.
  • The connector needs broad access for a narrow task.
  • The AI can take irreversible action without review.
  • There is no way to reconstruct what happened.

Connector review prompt

Use this before approving a new connector, expanding access, or letting an AI workflow read from or write to business systems.

Review this proposed AI connector before implementation.

Connector purpose:
[Describe the business task]

Systems connected:
[List apps, repositories, drives, databases, mailboxes, ticket queues, or APIs]

For each connected system, identify:
1. What identity the connector uses.
2. What data it can read.
3. What actions it can perform.
4. What data should be out of scope.
5. Whether retrieved content is trusted, untrusted, stale, private, regulated, or external.
6. What prompt-injection risks exist inside connected content.
7. What human approval is required before any write, send, delete, approval, deployment, or access change.
8. What logs, citations, source lists, and audit records must be retained.
9. What rollback or incident response path is available.
10. Whether a narrower permission scope would still complete the task.

Return:
- Recommended permission level
- Required controls before launch
- Risks that remain after controls
- Questions for data owners, security, legal, or platform teams
- A go, revise, or reject recommendation

Questions to ask before launch

These questions catch the most expensive surprises while they are still small enough to fit in a meeting note.

Question Why it matters Healthy answer
What did the AI see? Users and reviewers need source visibility to judge the answer. The response can show source names, record links, snippets, timestamps, and limits.
Whose permission was used? A shared connector identity can bypass normal user boundaries. Access maps to the user or to a narrow, reviewed service identity.
Can connected content instruct the AI? Emails, tickets, pages, and docs can contain hostile or accidental instructions. Retrieved content is treated as data. Tool rules and system instructions remain separate.
Can the connector change the world? Writing, sending, deleting, closing, approving, or deploying creates consequences. Live actions require approval, preview, logs, and rollback.
Who owns drift? Permissions, folders, groups, data sensitivity, and business rules change over time. There is a named owner, review schedule, and process for revoking or narrowing access.
Field guide takeaway: Connectors are not harmless pipes that pour knowledge into an AI cauldron. They are permissioned bridges into real systems, so they need narrow scope, source visibility, prompt-injection separation, action gates, audit trails, and named human owners before the bridge becomes part of daily work.
