Advanced AI Knowledge Field Guide

Security, Privacy, and Governance

AI use is a data-handling event, not just a chat. Prompts, files, screenshots, connectors, tool outputs, logs, generated code, and saved conversations can all carry secrets, customer data, internal strategy, or hostile instructions. Treat the whole workflow as a controlled boundary.

Protect secrets and customer data Detect prompt injection Respect vendor boundaries Escalate early

The AI security map

The danger is rarely one dramatic red button. It is usually a chain of small conveniences: paste a little too much, connect a little too broadly, trust one unchecked output, and suddenly the raccoon has admin.

🔑

Secrets and credentials

Never paste tokens, passwords, private keys, session cookies, signing certificates, recovery codes, database URLs, or cloud credentials into an unapproved AI path. Rotate anything exposed, even if the model “only saw it for a second.”

👥

Customer and personal data

Names, account details, support cases, health data, financial data, HR details, and identity records need classification first. Use placeholders or approved secure environments when real data is not required.

🧠

Internal knowledge and IP

Architecture diagrams, incident reports, pricing strategy, unreleased plans, private repositories, contracts, and legal analysis may be sensitive even when they contain no obvious secret string.

🧪

Generated output risk

AI can generate insecure code, false policy summaries, missing controls, unsafe runbooks, or overconfident answers. Review the output as if it came from a very fast intern with a fog machine.

Prompt injection is not only a chatbot problem

Hostile instructions can hide inside webpages, tickets, pull requests, emails, documents, logs, transcripts, or knowledge-base articles. When an AI system reads untrusted content and has useful tools, the content may try to steer the tool use. Treat outside text as evidence, not as instructions.

Untrusted contentAnything from users, vendors, websites, documents, repositories, comments, logs, or scraped sources.
Privileged toolsConnectors, email, calendars, repository write access, cloud commands, admin consoles, or deployment pipelines.
Safe patternSeparate reading from acting. Summarize first, verify intent, then require explicit approval before tool use.

The controlled AI boundary

Good governance does not mean “never use AI.” It means the path is known, approved, minimized, reviewed, and recoverable.

Six gates before sensitive AI work

Classify

Identify whether the material contains secrets, regulated data, customer data, internal confidential data, or public information.

Minimize

Remove fields that are not needed. Use synthetic examples, redacted logs, pseudonyms, or narrowed snippets whenever possible.

Choose the path

Use the approved AI tool, workspace, tenant, model, connector, and access mode for that data class.

Constrain tools

Limit repository access, connectors, command execution, browsing, file access, and write permissions to the exact task.

Review output

Check facts, code, citations, policies, commands, and assumptions before sending, merging, deploying, or sharing.

Record and escalate

Keep enough audit trail to explain what was used, who approved it, what changed, and where risks were escalated.

Vendor boundaries and tool choices

The question is not only “Which model is smartest?” The sharper question is “Where does the data go, who can access it, what is retained, and what tools can act on it?”

Before using a tool

  • Confirm the tool is approved for the data class.
  • Confirm whether prompts, files, and outputs are retained.
  • Confirm whether content may be used for model training.
  • Confirm tenant, region, workspace, and account boundaries.
  • Confirm connector permissions and inherited access.
  • Do not move sensitive work into a personal account.
  • Do not use a public model because the approved path is less convenient.
  • Do not grant broad connectors for a narrow task.
  • Do not upload whole repositories when a few files would answer the question.
  • Do not assume deletion means every copy, log, or derived artifact disappears.

Connector caution

Connectors can make AI much more useful, but they also turn “answer a question” into “search across systems with my permissions.” That is powerful, and power has hooves. Scope connectors to the minimum source set, prefer read-only access where possible, and require a review step before actions such as sending email, editing files, modifying tickets, opening pull requests, or deploying changes.

A connector should inherit accountability, not bypass it.

Escalation matrix

When in doubt, do not let the person closest to the deadline be the only control. Escalation is a safety rail, not a confession booth.

When to stop and get the approved path

Situation Primary risk Required behavior
Prompt or file includes secrets, tokens, passwords, private keys, certificates, or production credentials. Credential exposure, unauthorized access, account compromise. Do not paste. Use placeholders. If already exposed, stop, preserve context, notify the right team, and rotate or revoke.
Prompt includes customer records, support cases, HR data, financial details, medical data, or identity information. Privacy violation, regulatory exposure, contractual breach. Classify first. Use approved secure tooling, redaction, synthetic examples, or privacy review.
AI is asked to summarize legal, security, compliance, or policy obligations. False authority, missing obligations, stale rules. Require source review by accountable experts. Treat output as a draft, not a decision.
AI reads untrusted content and can use tools or connectors. Prompt injection, unintended actions, data exfiltration. Separate read and act phases. Disable or restrict tools. Require explicit human approval for actions.
AI output will be sent to customers, executives, regulators, vendors, or the public. Reputation, disclosure, incorrect commitments, legal exposure. Human review is mandatory. Verify facts, tone, approvals, and disclosure boundaries.
AI will modify repositories, infrastructure, access controls, deployment automation, or production workflows. Operational outage, privilege expansion, persistent defect. Use implementation plans, small diffs, validation commands, pull requests, and accountable approvers.

If something sensitive was exposed

Do not keep poking the raccoon to see if it bites again. The first move is containment.

1

Stop and preserve

Stop using the conversation, tool, connector, or agent path. Preserve the relevant prompt, file, output, timestamp, tool, account, and system involved so reviewers can understand what happened.

2

Revoke or rotate

Credentials, tokens, keys, cookies, and certificates should be treated as compromised. Rotate, revoke, invalidate sessions, and check logs for use after exposure.

3

Notify and learn

Escalate through the approved security, privacy, legal, or incident channel. Then update guidance so the next person gets a guardrail instead of a lecture.

Governance operating model

AI governance works best when it is boring in the right places: known roles, known gates, known logs, known escalation routes.

🧭

Define ownership

Assign who owns the business decision, data approval, technical change, security review, privacy review, and final communication. “The AI said so” is not an owner.

📋

Keep auditability

Record the prompt source, tool path, approvals, files changed, commands run, review notes, and final decision. Logs should explain the decision trail without becoming a data landfill.

🚦

Use approval gates

Require stronger gates for high-risk data, public communication, production change, external sharing, incident response, or anything that could affect access, money, users, or legal obligations.

Field guide takeaway: Secure AI use is not about refusing useful tools. It is about making sure the right data enters the right tool through the right path, under the right permissions, with the right human still responsible when the answer leaves the screen.
