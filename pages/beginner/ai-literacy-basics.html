AI Literacy Basics

Learn where AI can help, where it is unreliable, and why it should be treated as an assistant rather than an authority.

Expertise: Beginner Audience: General business users Use when: You are starting with AI tools Version: 1.0

Who this is for

This guide is for people who want to use AI for everyday work such as drafting, summarizing, brainstorming, organizing notes, or preparing first-pass explanations.

What you will learn

  • What AI tools are useful for.
  • Why AI output still needs review.
  • When to slow down, verify, or ask for expert help.

AI is useful, but it is not an authority

AI tools are good at producing language, organizing information, finding patterns, and helping you think through options. They can draft an email, summarize a meeting note, convert rough notes into a checklist, or explain a topic in plainer language.

They are not reliable sources of truth by themselves. AI can misunderstand context, invent details, miss recent changes, or sound confident while being wrong. Use AI as a capable assistant that speeds up work, not as the final reviewer or decision-maker.

Good use and risky use

Good use

Ask AI to draft a first version, simplify wording, create a checklist, brainstorm options, or explain a non-sensitive topic. Then review the output before using it.

Risky use

Ask AI to make a final decision, interpret policy without review, produce customer-facing claims without checking, or handle sensitive information in an unapproved tool.

Try this prompt

Use this when you want a plain-language explanation and a review checklist.

Explain this topic for a beginner: [topic]. Use plain language. Include what it is useful for, what it is not reliable for, and what a human should review before using the output. End with three questions I should ask before trusting the result.

Key takeaway

AI can accelerate thinking and drafting, but people remain responsible for checking facts, protecting information, and deciding what is safe to use.
