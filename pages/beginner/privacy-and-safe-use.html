Privacy and Safe Use

AI is useful because it can work with the context you give it. That is also the privacy trap: every prompt is a tiny delivery truck. Before you load it, know what can travel, what must be disguised, and what requires an approved road.

Level: Beginner Rule: Do not paste sensitive data casually Skill: Replace details with safe placeholders

The core idea

Treat an AI prompt as a work request, not a diary page. The model does not need every private detail to help. It often needs the shape of the problem, the goal, the constraints, and a sanitized sample that preserves the pattern without exposing the secret.

Give AI enough context to solve the task, not enough data to become the incident.

Three safe-use instincts

Before using AI, pause long enough to classify the material. That tiny pause prevents most privacy goblins from escaping the sock drawer.

Assume prompts can leave a footprint. Some tools store prompts, attach them to accounts, use connectors, or retain logs for review, safety, audit, support, or administration.
Separate the pattern from the identity. AI can usually help with “a customer disputed an invoice” without knowing the customer’s name, account number, address, or payment details.
Use the approved path for real data. If the task needs confidential, regulated, customer, employee, security, or production data, use the organization-approved tool and workflow.

The red, amber, green data lanes

Not every prompt has the same risk. Use the lane that matches the data, not the convenience of the moment.

Stop

Do not paste

These details should stay out unless a specifically approved tool and process allows them.

High-risk content

  • Passwords, API keys, tokens, private keys, certificates, recovery codes, or session cookies.
  • Personal information about customers, employees, patients, students, donors, or applicants.
  • Payment card data, bank details, tax identifiers, payroll information, or government identifiers.
  • Confidential contracts, legal advice, investigations, disciplinary matters, or board-level material.
  • Security incidents, vulnerabilities, logs, hostnames, IPs, or architecture details that expose attack paths.
Sanitize

Use placeholders

Keep the structure of the problem while removing the identifying or sensitive pieces.

Replace with safe tokens

  • Replace names with [CUSTOMER], [EMPLOYEE], or [VENDOR].
  • Replace IDs with [ACCOUNT_ID], [TICKET_ID], or [ORDER_NUMBER].
  • Replace locations with region-level context, such as [CITY] or [REGION].
  • Replace exact money, dates, and counts if precision is not required.
  • Summarize proprietary logic instead of pasting internal source material verbatim.
Proceed

Usually safe

These uses are generally lower-risk when they do not include confidential details.

Low-risk tasks

  • Rewriting public text for clarity, tone, structure, or audience.
  • Brainstorming generic options, agendas, checklists, training outlines, or examples.
  • Explaining general concepts with no private business context attached.
  • Creating a reusable template with placeholders instead of real values.
  • Reviewing a sanitized draft for missing context, ambiguity, or unsupported claims.

The safe-use loop

Use this five-step loop before pasting material into an AI tool. It is quick, repeatable, and much cheaper than explaining later why a secret took a field trip.

Classify the data

Ask what kind of information you are about to include: public, internal, confidential, regulated, personal, security-sensitive, or production-sensitive.

Strip the identity

Remove names, account details, exact identifiers, locations, proprietary labels, and any detail that is not needed for the task.

Preserve the pattern

Keep enough structure for the AI to help: the goal, the sequence of events, the categories involved, and the decision you need to make.

Choose the tool

If real sensitive data is necessary, use only the approved enterprise, legal, security, or governed path. Public tools are not a shortcut.

Review the output

Check that the answer did not invent facts, reveal hidden assumptions, recreate sensitive details, or suggest an action outside your authority.

The placeholder method

Good anonymization is not just deleting names. It means replacing sensitive details with labels that preserve meaning. The AI should understand the role of each detail without seeing the real detail.

[PERSON_A]Use for a person when the relationship matters, but the identity does not.
[CUSTOMER_TYPE]Use for segment or role, such as residential customer, enterprise client, donor, or applicant.
[SYSTEM]Use for a platform, application, or internal service when the exact name is not needed.
[SENSITIVE_DETAIL]Use when the fact matters, but the value itself should not travel.

Risky prompt

Rewrite this message to the customer: “Hi Amanda, your account 991-42-7712 was suspended after the payment failed on card ending 4022.  Your address on file is 123 Example Street...”

Safer prompt

Rewrite this customer-service message so it is clear, polite, and concise.  Use placeholders and do not add new commitments.

Context: [CUSTOMER] has a suspended account because [PAYMENT_METHOD] failed.  We need them to update payment details through the official portal.

Draft: “Your account is suspended because payment failed.  Please update your payment method in the portal.”

When an approved path is required

Some work is not solved by better placeholders. If the task genuinely requires sensitive data, switch from casual prompting to governed use.

1

Customer or employee data

Anything tied to a real person, account, address, complaint, HR case, health matter, payment, identity, or support history needs extra care.

2

Confidential business material

Contracts, pricing, strategy, unreleased announcements, financial details, vendor negotiations, and legal material belong in approved workflows.

3

Security or production context

Incident notes, vulnerabilities, logs, network maps, credentials, IAM details, and production configs need security-approved handling.

4

Automated access

Connectors, file access, inbox access, code repositories, ticket systems, and databases turn AI from a chat box into a tool with reach.

Fast decision matrix

Use this table when you are not sure whether the prompt is safe enough.

Situation Safer action Why
You want help improving a public announcement. Paste the public draft and ask for clarity, tone, and structure. The content is already intended for public release, though facts still need human review.
You want to summarize a customer complaint. Replace the customer, account, dates, contact details, and exact identifiers with placeholders. The AI can help with structure and response tone without seeing the customer’s identity.
You need AI to analyze real records or tickets. Use the approved enterprise tool, approved connector, or approved workflow. The task needs access controls, logging, retention rules, and organizational accountability.
You want help with an error log. Redact hostnames, IPs, tokens, usernames, paths, request IDs, and customer identifiers first. Logs often hide secrets and infrastructure maps in plain sight.
You want AI to draft policy, legal, HR, security, or medical wording. Ask for a general structure, then route the result through the accountable reviewer. AI can draft language, but it cannot approve advice or own the decision.
You are not sure whether the data is sensitive. Do not paste it yet. Ask for a sanitization approach using only a description of the data category. Uncertainty is a signal to slow down, not a coupon for speed-running risk.
Field guide takeaway: Privacy-safe AI use is not about being afraid of the tool. It is about controlling the cargo. Keep secrets, identities, and regulated data out of casual prompts; use placeholders when the pattern is enough; and require approved paths when the real data must be involved.
