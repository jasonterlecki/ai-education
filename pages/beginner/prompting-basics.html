Prompting Basics

Better prompts give AI a job, the right context, useful boundaries, examples, and a clear output format.

Expertise: Beginner Audience: General business users Use when: You need more useful AI responses Version: 1.0

Who this is for

This guide is for anyone who has tried a short prompt and received a vague, generic, or hard-to-use answer.

What you will learn

  • The five parts of a stronger prompt.
  • How to improve a weak prompt.
  • How to ask for a format that is easier to review.

A simple prompt structure

Goal: Say what you want done. Context: Explain the audience, situation, and background. Constraints: State boundaries such as tone, length, privacy, or policy limits. Examples: Show a sample style, input, or pattern when helpful. Output format: Ask for bullets, a table, a checklist, a draft email, or another reviewable shape.

Before and after

Weak prompt

Write something about AI for my team.

Stronger prompt

Draft a 200-word note for non-technical staff explaining three safe ways to use AI at work. Keep the tone practical, avoid hype, do not mention confidential data, and end with a review checklist.

Good use and risky use

Good use

Use prompting structure to create drafts, outlines, summaries, checklists, and options that you can inspect and adjust.

Risky use

Use a vague prompt for important work, then accept the answer without checking facts, tone, assumptions, or missing context.

Try this prompt

Replace the bracketed text with your real, non-sensitive task details.

Goal: [what I need]. Context: [audience, situation, and background]. Constraints: [tone, length, privacy limits, things to avoid]. Examples: [optional example or style reference]. Output format: [bullets, table, checklist, draft email, or summary]. Before answering, list any assumptions you are making.

Key takeaway

A strong prompt does not make AI automatically correct. It makes the answer easier to understand, review, and improve.
