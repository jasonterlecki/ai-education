AI Use Case Intake

An AI idea should not go straight from hallway enthusiasm to production workflow. Intake turns the first spark into a structured decision: what problem is being solved, who owns it, what data is involved, how risky it is, how success will be measured, and whether AI is actually the right tool for the job.

Intake is where excitement becomes accountable.

A good intake process does not exist to smother ideas under a policy quilt. It exists to separate useful opportunities from unsafe shortcuts, unclear ownership, unsupported promises, duplicate work, and demos that look shiny because nobody has asked where the data came from.

The intake question is not “can we use AI here?” The better question is “what decision, workflow, evidence, owner, and control would make this use case safe enough to try?”

What intake should produce

1
A clear problem statementWhat pain exists, who feels it, what happens today, and what would improve.
2
A risk and data classificationWhat data is touched, what decisions are influenced, and what harm could occur.
3
A recommended next stepReject, redesign, research, prototype, pilot, or route to formal review.

The five intake gates

The gates are not meant to be bureaucratic stone gargoyles. They are fast filters that catch weak assumptions before a team spends weeks building the wrong thing with excellent typography.

Value

Is there a real problem worth solving? The use case should name the user, pain, current workaround, expected improvement, and why AI adds value over simpler automation or process cleanup.

Fit

Does the task match what AI is good at? AI is strong for drafting, classification, summarization, search assistance, pattern spotting, and decision support. It is weaker when exact truth, hidden context, or deterministic control is mandatory.

Data

What information will the system read, write, retain, or expose? Intake should classify sensitivity, source of truth, retention needs, masking options, and whether approved tooling is required.

Risk

What could go wrong if the AI is wrong, biased, stale, overconfident, manipulated, or misused? Risk is shaped by autonomy, reversibility, audience, data sensitivity, and business impact.

Readiness

Is there an owner, pilot plan, metric, review process, fallback path, and support model? If nobody owns the result after the demo, the demo is a glitter pumpkin.

A practical intake flow

Use intake to route ideas quickly. Low-risk ideas should not drown in paperwork, and high-risk ideas should not sneak through because they had a catchy slide title.

SubmitCapture the problem, sponsor, users, workflow, data, desired output, and expected benefit.
ScreenCheck for duplicate work, obvious policy blockers, data sensitivity, and whether AI is even needed.
ClassifyAssign risk tier using data, autonomy, reversibility, decision impact, and external exposure.
DesignDefine human review, tool boundaries, source of truth, metrics, pilot scope, and failure handling.
DecideReject, research, prototype, pilot, or escalate to security, privacy, legal, or architecture review.
TrackKeep status, owners, evidence, decisions, approvals, risks, and lessons learned visible.

The intake questions that matter

Good questions keep the process grounded. The goal is not to interrogate the submitter with a clipboard thunderstorm, but to expose the assumptions that change the decision.

?

Problem and workflow

What workflow will change? Who performs it today? How often does it happen? What is slow, expensive, risky, inconsistent, or frustrating?

↔

AI role

Will AI draft, summarize, retrieve, classify, recommend, monitor, execute, or decide? The word “assist” is not enough. Name the actual behavior.

◎

Users and affected people

Who uses the output, who is affected by it, who can challenge it, and who is accountable when the output is wrong?

D

Data and sources

What data enters the system, where does it come from, who owns it, how current is it, and what is the source of truth when sources disagree?

!

Risk and failure

What harm happens if the output is wrong, incomplete, biased, leaked, stale, manipulated, or followed too literally?

✓

Success and evidence

What metric proves the use case is useful? What evidence proves it is safe enough? What would make the pilot stop?

Intake routing matrix

Signal Likely route Why Evidence required
Low-risk productivity helper Prototype Examples include summarizing non-sensitive notes, drafting internal text, or creating first-pass checklists with human review. Use case owner, approved tool, data boundary, and review expectation.
Uses internal data or repository context Controlled pilot Useful, but source quality, access controls, prompt injection, and accidental disclosure need attention. Data classification, access model, source of truth, logging, and reviewer role.
Affects customers, employees, money, access, safety, or legal position Formal review Higher blast radius means the organization needs privacy, security, legal, risk, and human oversight before pilots become real workflows. Risk assessment, human-in-the-loop design, fallback path, monitoring, and approval record.
Autonomous action in shared or production systems Escalate or block State-changing automation needs permission design, exact tool boundaries, validation evidence, rollback, and named ownership. Permission ladder, MCP/tool review, runbook, approval gates, audit logs, and rollback plan.
Unclear problem, no owner, no metric, or “we just want AI” Redesign AI should not be a fog machine for an undefined process. Clarify the problem before selecting the technology. Problem statement, current workflow, target users, measurable success, and non-goals.

Evidence to collect before saying yes

The intake record should be useful months later. It should explain why the idea was approved, changed, paused, or rejected without requiring everyone to remember the meeting weather.

P

Problem evidence

Volume, time spent, error rate, user frustration, operational delay, cost, missed opportunity, or compliance pain.

D

Data evidence

Data owner, classification, source of truth, freshness, access method, retention needs, and redaction strategy.

R

Risk evidence

Failure modes, affected people, reversibility, autonomy level, external exposure, and required reviewers.

M

Measurement evidence

Success metric, baseline, evaluation method, sample cases, pilot duration, stop conditions, and owner sign-off.

Artifacts intake should create

Intake is a decision trail, not a memory game. These artifacts make the work reviewable, repeatable, and easier to move from idea to pilot without dropping the compass.

1

Use case brief

One page covering problem, users, workflow, desired output, AI role, value hypothesis, data sources, and non-goals.

2

Risk tier record

Classification based on data sensitivity, autonomy, decision impact, reversibility, affected people, and exposure.

3

Review map

Who must review the use case: business owner, technical owner, data owner, security, privacy, legal, HR, compliance, or architecture.

4

Pilot plan

Scope, users, tools, data boundaries, success metrics, evaluation cases, feedback loop, fallback, and stop conditions.

5

Control list

Human review, logging, permission boundaries, source citations, approved models, retention rules, and escalation path.

6

Decision record

Approved, rejected, redesigned, or escalated, with reasons, assumptions, owners, expiry date, and next review point.

Failure modes intake should catch early

The cheapest time to catch a bad AI idea is before it gets a dashboard, a champion, and a heroic launch name.

!

Solution-first thinking

The request starts with “we need a chatbot” instead of a problem, workflow, user, and measurable pain.

!

Invisible data risk

The idea sounds harmless until someone notices it needs customer records, HR data, credentials, contracts, or sensitive tickets.

!

No human owner

The team wants AI to produce an answer, but nobody owns review, correction, support, adoption, or the consequences of error.

?

Unmeasurable success

“Better productivity” is not enough. Intake needs a baseline and a signal: time saved, quality improved, risk reduced, or cycle time shortened.

?

Pilot that cannot stop

A pilot without stop conditions slowly becomes production by sediment. Define failure triggers and expiry dates.

?

Review theater

Approval happens because a committee saw a demo, not because evidence showed the use case is safe, useful, and owned.

Reusable AI use case intake prompt

Use this when someone proposes a new AI use case and the idea needs fast but serious triage.

Help me intake this proposed AI use case. Use case name: Sponsor or owner: Target users: Current workflow: Problem or pain: Proposed AI role: Data involved: Systems or tools involved: Expected benefit: Known risks or concerns: Desired timeline: Assess: 1. Problem clarity and whether AI is a good fit. 2. Data sensitivity and source-of-truth issues. 3. Risk tier based on autonomy, decision impact, reversibility, affected people, and external exposure. 4. Required reviewers or approvers. 5. Minimum controls for a safe prototype or pilot. 6. Success metrics and stop conditions. 7. Whether to reject, redesign, research, prototype, pilot, or escalate. Return a concise intake decision with reasons, missing information, and next actions.

Fast intake checklist

  • Is the problem specific enough to evaluate?
  • Is AI clearly better than simpler automation, search, workflow cleanup, or documentation?
  • Is there a named business owner and technical owner?
  • Are data sources, sensitivity, retention, and access boundaries known?
  • Is the AI output advisory, drafted, reviewed, or actioned automatically?
  • Are affected people, failure modes, and reversibility understood?
  • Are success metrics, pilot limits, and stop conditions defined?
  • Does this require security, privacy, legal, HR, compliance, or architecture review?

Field guide takeaway: AI use case intake is the front door where enthusiasm gets a clipboard, a map, and a responsible adult. A strong intake process names the problem, owner, data, AI role, risk tier, success metric, review gates, and stop conditions before the idea becomes a pilot, so teams can move quickly without letting shiny demos sneak around governance, privacy, security, or common sense.
