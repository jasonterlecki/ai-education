Implementation Plans and Specs

Create a PLAN.md, specification, migration path, or runbook before controlled delivery begins. AI can help draft the map, but the map must make the work reviewable, bounded, testable, and recoverable.

Why this page exists

AI makes it dangerously easy to leap from “interesting idea” to “agent, go change files.” That jump feels fast until the first hidden assumption becomes architecture, a migration path is invented mid-flight, or nobody can explain what changed. A good plan slows the launch just enough to prevent the delivery rocket from becoming a confetti cannon.

The practical rule

When AI output will guide changes to real systems, shared documents, operational workflows, customer-facing behavior, permissions, data, or production processes, require a controlled delivery artifact first.

No plan, no controlled delivery. First draw the bridge, then cross it.

The four planning artifacts

These are not paperwork goblins. Each one answers a different delivery question and keeps AI, humans, and reviewers from stepping on each other’s boots.

P

PLAN.md

A phased implementation map. It explains what happens first, what waits, what must not be touched, and how each phase proves it is done.

Best for AI agents
S

Specification

A precise description of the desired behavior, interfaces, permissions, constraints, data rules, error states, and acceptance criteria.

Best for alignment
M

Migration path

A controlled route from current state to target state, including compatibility, backfill, coexistence, cutover, validation, and cleanup.

Best for change
R

Runbook

An operating guide for humans: commands, checks, owners, expected signals, failure symptoms, escalation paths, and rollback steps.

Best for operations

What goes wrong without a plan

The AI may still produce something that looks competent. That is the trap. The missing pieces hide in the spaces between confident paragraphs.

  • Scope expands because nobody named the non-goals.
  • The AI touches protected files because nobody fenced them off.
  • Implementation order is chosen by convenience instead of dependency.
  • Testing becomes a final flourish instead of a phase gate.
  • Migration gets treated as “copy the data somehow” rather than a reversible path.
  • Rollback is written after deployment, which is basically sewing a parachute after jumping.

What a good plan makes visible

A good plan turns fog into inspectable terrain. Reviewers should be able to see what will change, what will not change, and where risk is being held.

  • The target outcome and the reason it matters.
  • The current system, document, workflow, or process being changed.
  • The exact boundaries of the work, including protected areas.
  • The sequence of phases and what each phase proves.
  • The validation commands, review gates, screenshots, samples, or acceptance checks.
  • The rollback, recovery, and “stop here” conditions.

Anatomy of a useful PLAN.md

PLAN.md should be usable by a human reviewer and by an AI coding agent. It should not be a wish list wearing a hard hat.

1

Outcome and success definition

State what must be true when the work is finished, and how the team will recognize success.

2

Current-state inventory

Name the existing files, workflows, routes, APIs, data sources, dependencies, and assumptions that matter.

3

Scope, non-goals, and protected areas

Tell the AI and reviewers what is allowed, what is explicitly out of scope, and what must not be modified.

4

Phased execution

Break the work into bounded slices that can be implemented, reviewed, tested, and stopped independently.

5

Acceptance criteria

Define the observable behavior, sample cases, permissions, edge cases, and review evidence expected for each phase.

6

Validation and review gates

List linting, tests, smoke checks, screenshots, dry runs, peer reviews, and approvals required before moving forward.

7

Rollout and rollback

Explain how the change is released, how failure is detected, and how the team returns to a known-good state.

8

Open questions and assumptions

Keep uncertainty visible. Do not bury unknowns inside confident implementation language.

Migration path: the controlled runway

Migration is where plans earn their snacks. A safe path keeps old and new behavior understandable while the change is being introduced.

Inventory

Identify current data, routes, users, permissions, automations, downstream consumers, and hidden dependencies.

Compatibility

Decide how legacy and new behavior coexist, including feature flags, parallel routes, or read-only preview modes.

Prepare

Create schema, config, files, docs, or admin controls without switching user-facing behavior yet.

Move in slices

Migrate a small, reversible segment first. Verify the pattern before repeating it at scale.

Cut over

Switch traffic, ownership, or behavior only after acceptance checks and rollback steps are ready.

Retire safely

Remove legacy paths only after monitoring confirms stability and the rollback window has closed.

Specification checklist

A specification tells people what should exist. It should be plain enough for stakeholders and precise enough for builders.

Spec area Question it answers
Users and roles Who can use this, who can approve it, and who must not have access?
Inputs and outputs What data enters, what result leaves, and what format must be preserved?
Rules and constraints What must the system always do, never do, or ask a human to confirm?
Error and edge cases What happens when information is missing, conflicting, stale, invalid, or unavailable?
Acceptance evidence What examples, screenshots, tests, or review notes prove the work is complete?

Runbook checklist

A runbook tells people how to operate the change after the shiny build music stops.

  • Who owns the workflow, system, document, or release?
  • What commands, buttons, approvals, or dashboards are used?
  • What normal behavior looks like after launch.
  • What warning signs mean the change should pause.
  • How to roll back, restore, disable, or escalate.
  • What should be logged for audit, review, or future troubleshooting.

Which artifact should you ask AI for?

Use the artifact that matches the risk. Asking for the wrong document is how teams get beautiful nonsense in a laminated folder.

Situation Ask for Why
The idea is still fuzzy and stakeholders need options. Vibe brief or concept brief. You need direction first, not delivery instructions.
Someone will implement changes across files, pages, infrastructure, or workflows. PLAN.md. The work needs phases, guardrails, validation, and review checkpoints.
People disagree on what the feature or process should do. Specification. The team needs shared behavior, constraints, and acceptance criteria before building.
Current behavior must move to a new model without breaking the old model immediately. Migration path. Compatibility, cutover, rollback, and cleanup are the real work.
A human will operate, monitor, support, or recover the change. Runbook. The team needs repeatable steps, ownership, warning signs, and recovery instructions.

Prompt pattern

Use this when a conversation is moving from exploration to controlled delivery. It forces the AI to produce something reviewable before anyone asks it to execute.

A plan is not “extra.” It is the container that keeps speed from leaking into risk.

Reusable prompt

Create a controlled delivery plan for the following work: [describe the work].

Before proposing implementation steps, identify:
- current-state assumptions that must be verified
- scope and non-goals
- protected files, data, systems, or workflows
- stakeholders, owners, reviewers, and approvers
- privacy, security, permission, and operational risks

Produce the output as:
1. Executive summary
2. Current-state inventory
3. Target-state specification
4. Phased PLAN.md-style implementation path
5. Migration or coexistence plan, if needed
6. Validation and review gates per phase
7. Rollout plan
8. Rollback and recovery plan
9. Open questions and assumptions that require human confirmation

Do not write implementation code yet.  The goal is a reviewable plan.

Red flags in AI-generated plans

These are the smells that tell you the document is plan-shaped fog, not a delivery map.

!

Everything is Phase 1

The plan has no controlled sequence, no stopping points, and no safe review rhythm.

!

No named non-goals

If nothing is out of scope, the AI may decide everything is an invitation.

!

No validation evidence

“Test thoroughly” is not a test plan. Name the checks, examples, and review artifacts.

!

Rollback appears last-minute

If rollback is vague, rollout is not ready. The escape hatch should be designed before launch.

Field guide takeaway: Implementation plans and specs turn AI from a persuasive idea fountain into a controlled delivery partner. The plan does not replace judgment. It gives judgment a surface to inspect, challenge, approve, and safely execute.
