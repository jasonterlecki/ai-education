The communication guardrails

Email is not just wording. It can create expectations, promises, obligations, escalation paths, public records, and reputational confetti. The prompt must protect the message before it polishes the message.

1

Facts stay fixed

Names, dates, amounts, owners, deadlines, policies, incident details, and decisions must remain anchored to the provided source material.

2

No invented commitments

The AI must not promise timelines, approvals, refunds, compensation, support, exceptions, or follow-up unless those commitments are explicitly provided.

3

Tone has a job

Warm, firm, apologetic, neutral, executive, friendly, or direct should be chosen because the situation requires it, not because the draft wants perfume.

4

Ownership stays human

The sender approves the content, owns the relationship, and decides whether the message is appropriate. AI can draft, but it does not stand behind the message.

5

Uncertainty is visible

Missing context, risky phrasing, unsupported claims, and possible escalation issues should be surfaced before the message is sent.

The practical rule

Do not ask AI to “write a better email” unless you are comfortable with it deciding what better means. Give it the audience, the goal, the facts, the boundaries, and the review standard.

AI may improve the wording. It may not improve reality by inventing a nicer one.

Reusable prompt 
Role:
Act as a careful communication editor and risk-aware second reader.

Goal:
Improve the clarity, tone, and structure of the message below while preserving the intended meaning.

Audience:
Recipient or audience: [person, team, customer, vendor, executive, public audience]
Relationship/context: [why we are communicating, history, sensitivity, power dynamic]
Desired outcome: [inform, request action, de-escalate, explain, confirm, decline, summarize, follow up]

Facts that must remain unchanged:
- [name/date/amount/policy/decision/owner/deadline]
- [anything the AI must not reinterpret]

Tone and style:
Use a tone that is [professional, warm, firm, concise, empathetic, neutral, executive, etc.].
Avoid sounding [defensive, vague, robotic, over-apologetic, casual, legalistic, etc.].

Boundaries:
- Do not invent facts, dates, names, numbers, policies, approvals, or decisions.
- Do not create new commitments, promises, refunds, compensation, timelines, exceptions, or follow-up actions.
- Do not apologize, accept fault, assign blame, or imply liability unless the source text explicitly says to do so.
- Do not change the sender's position, authority, or decision.
- If information is missing, list it under "Questions before sending" instead of guessing.
- If wording could create risk or an unintended commitment, flag it under "Risk notes."

Output format:
1. Revised message
2. What changed and why
3. Risk notes
4. Questions before sending

Draft or notes:
[paste approved draft, notes, or bullet points]

Use it as a review loop, not a send button

The safest workflow is draft, inspect, revise, then send only after the accountable human confirms the facts and intent. Do not let polished language sneak past review wearing a tiny fake mustache.

Prepare the facts Separate source facts from desired tone. Use placeholders for sensitive names, identifiers, account numbers, or private details.
Generate the draft Ask for the revised message plus change notes, risk notes, and questions. A naked draft is harder to inspect.
Review the risks Check for accidental commitments, altered meaning, unsupported certainty, privacy leakage, and tone mismatch.
Send as yourself The human sender decides whether the message is appropriate, complete, approved, and ready for the recipient.

Weak vs. reviewable communication prompts

The weak version gives AI a sponge and a bucket of vibes. The stronger version gives it rails, labels, and a place to put uncertainty.

Stronger prompt

This tells AI how to help without letting it become a tiny unauthorized spokesperson hiding in the stationery drawer.

Act as a careful communication editor.

Goal: Improve this vendor follow-up email for clarity and professionalism.

Audience: External vendor contact.  We need a status update without escalating tone.

Facts that must remain unchanged:
- The original request was sent on April 8.
- We need the updated quote before Friday.
- No approval has been granted yet.

Tone: Professional, direct, and calm.

Boundaries:
- Do not imply we will purchase anything.
- Do not create a new deadline beyond "before Friday."
- Do not apologize or assign blame.
- If anything is unclear, list it as a question.

Output:
1. Revised email
2. What changed and why
3. Risk notes
4. Questions before sending

Human review checklist

Before sending, review the communication as if the recipient might quote it later, forward it upward, act on it, or misunderstand it with great enthusiasm.

Facts Check names, dates, amounts, ticket numbers, links, decisions, policies, and all details that sound precise.
Commitments Look for accidental promises, deadlines, approvals, ownership changes, exceptions, or support obligations.
Tone Make sure the message is appropriate for the relationship, sensitivity, audience, and desired outcome.
Authority Confirm the sender is allowed to say this and that any required approval path has actually happened.

Communication risk matrix

Use stricter prompting and review when the message can change expectations, obligations, approvals, money, customer trust, or public posture.

Communication type Add this to the prompt Review especially for
Routine internal update. Audience, goal, tone, key facts, and requested action. Clarity, correct dates, ownership, and whether the call to action is obvious.
Customer or client response. Approved facts, support boundaries, escalation path, and what must not be promised. Commitments, privacy exposure, tone, blame, compensation, and unsupported assurances.
Incident or outage communication. Confirmed timeline, approved wording, audience, known impact, and escalation owner. Unverified root cause, liability language, timelines, public claims, and premature certainty.
Executive summary email. Decision needed, confidence level, source material, risks, and constraints. Over-smoothing, hidden assumptions, missing tradeoffs, and ambiguous asks.
Vendor negotiation or procurement. Negotiation posture, non-commitment language, approval status, and commercial boundaries. Accidental purchase intent, pricing claims, deadlines, concessions, and authorization language.
Public or broad-audience message. Approved source text, brand tone, review path, prohibited claims, and spokesperson boundaries. Public promises, reputational risk, policy mismatch, accessibility, and missing approvals.
Prompt-library takeaway: AI can make communication clearer, calmer, and easier to review. It should not invent facts, make promises, change decisions, or wear the sender’s accountability like a borrowed coat.
