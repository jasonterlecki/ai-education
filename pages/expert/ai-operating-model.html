AI Operating Model

An AI operating model turns scattered enthusiasm into a governed system: who may use AI, which tools are approved, which work is allowed, which controls are required, and where people escalate when the work becomes risky, uncertain, or public.

The six questions every AI program must answer

An operating model is not a slogan. It is the answer key people use when they are deciding whether AI belongs in a task, which tool is safe, and who owns the consequences.

Who can use AI?

Define eligible users, roles, training expectations, champions, tool owners, privileged users, and people who need additional approval before using connected or automated systems.

Which tools are approved?

Maintain a visible catalog of approved assistants, coding agents, connected knowledge tools, automation tools, and prohibited or unreviewed public services.

Which work is allowed?

Separate low-risk drafting from regulated decisions, production changes, customer data work, financial actions, public communications, and security-sensitive operations.

Which controls apply?

Map the work to required controls: data handling, review, approval, testing, logging, monitoring, rollback, audit evidence, and human accountability.

Who approves exceptions?

Define how teams request new tools, risky use cases, connector access, automation privileges, or temporary exceptions, and who can say yes.

When do people escalate?

Give employees clear stop signs for secrets, customer data, legal risk, incidents, prompt injection, suspicious output, model failure, or actions that affect real users.

The operating loop

The loop should be boring in the best way: intake, classify, route, control, execute, review, improve. Tiny governance gears, big safety machine.

Intake the use case

Capture the goal, users, data, tools, expected output, business owner, and whether the result will influence real decisions or systems.

Classify the risk

Score the work by data sensitivity, external exposure, automation level, system access, financial impact, and reversibility.

Choose the path

Route the work to an approved tool and the correct mode: assistive drafting, analysis, code support, controlled automation, or prohibited use.

Attach controls

Add the required reviews, privacy handling, validation commands, approval gates, monitoring, audit records, and rollback expectations.

Operate and review

Use AI within the defined boundary. Humans review outputs, own decisions, document approvals, and verify high-risk facts or changes.

Improve the model

Feed lessons, incidents, adoption friction, false starts, prompt failures, and successful patterns back into training and guidance.

Roles that keep the model from turning into fog

Good governance gives people names, not vibes. Every serious AI program needs visible ownership for decisions, tools, data, controls, training, and incidents.

S

Executive sponsor

Sets direction, resolves priority conflicts, funds the program, and makes sure AI governance is treated as operational reality rather than decorative policy.

O

AI program owner

Maintains the operating model, approved-use catalog, training roadmap, intake process, exception queue, metrics, and governance cadence.

T

Tool owner

Owns configuration, access, vendor settings, connectors, retention, logging, permission model, release notes, and tool-specific user guidance.

D

Data owner

Decides whether data may be used, what masking is required, which retrieval sources are authoritative, and when access must be limited.

R

Risk reviewers

Security, privacy, legal, compliance, and architecture reviewers define control requirements for sensitive, regulated, or system-impacting work.

U

Users and managers

Use AI within approved boundaries, review outputs, document decisions, escalate problems, and keep responsibility attached to the human chain.

The practical rule

If people need to guess whether AI is allowed, the operating model is not finished. The model should make the safe path easier to find than the risky shortcut.

Do not govern AI only by tool name. Govern it by user, task, data, access, action, evidence, and escalation.

Reusable operating-model prompt

Create an AI operating model for this organization or team.

Include:
- who can use AI and any training requirements
- approved tool categories and prohibited tool use
- allowed, conditional, and restricted work
- data handling rules by sensitivity
- review and approval requirements by risk tier
- controls for coding agents, connected tools, and automation
- escalation triggers and accountable owners
- required evidence, logs, metrics, and audit artifacts
- exception process for new tools or unusual use cases

Make the guidance practical enough that employees can decide what to do without guessing.

Artifacts the operating model should produce

The operating model should leave durable evidence behind. If the guidance exists only in meetings and heroic hallway memory, it will evaporate the first time someone needs it under pressure.

  • Approved tool registry: tool names, owners, allowed users, data boundaries, retention settings, and connector status.
  • Use-case register: business owner, purpose, data used, risk tier, controls, approval status, and review date.
  • Data handling guide: what can be pasted, what needs placeholders, what needs approved paths, and what is prohibited.
  • Risk-tier matrix: controls by task type, autonomy level, data class, audience, and reversibility.
  • Prompt and output guidance: examples of safe prompting, verification, review expectations, and forbidden shortcuts.
  • Agent and automation rules: repository permissions, stop points, validation, monitoring, rollback, and human approval gates.
  • Exception process: how to request a new tool, unusual data use, higher autonomy, or temporary deviation from the rules.
  • Incident and escalation path: who to contact for leakage, prompt injection, suspicious behavior, bad output, or tool misuse.
  • Training plan: role-based onboarding, refresher material, champions, office hours, and feedback loops.
  • Metrics dashboard: adoption, value, risk reviews, incidents, exceptions, control failures, and training completion.

Controls by AI capability level

Capability What can go wrong Minimum operating controls
General chat assistant Users paste sensitive data, trust hallucinated facts, or publish polished nonsense because it sounds confident. Privacy guidance, safe prompting examples, verification checklist, human accountability, and source review for factual claims.
Connected knowledge assistant The assistant retrieves stale, over-permissioned, incorrect, or confidential material and presents it as authoritative. Source ownership, freshness rules, permission alignment, citation expectations, knowledge review, and content retirement process.
Coding agent The agent edits broad files, runs unsafe commands, misses tests, changes behavior outside scope, or leaks secrets in logs. Repository instructions, least privilege, branch isolation, checkpointed execution, validation commands, code review, and protected-file rules.
Workflow automation The system acts on bad input, repeats errors at scale, misses alert signals, or takes action without human approval. Approval gates, observability, monitoring, rollback, audit trails, rate limits, escalation triggers, and manual override.
Decision-support system People confuse recommendation with decision, overlook bias or missing context, or automate a judgment that requires accountable review. Clear decision owner, explainability expectations, evaluation, appeal path, human review, legal/privacy assessment, and documented limitations.
Field guide takeaway: An AI operating model is the map, the guardrail, and the emergency flare. It does not slow responsible teams down. It keeps them from having to invent policy while the cart is already rolling downhill with production data in the back.
