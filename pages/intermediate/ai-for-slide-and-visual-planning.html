Intermediate Field Guide

AI for Slide and Visual Planning

Use AI to plan the story, audience journey, evidence, visuals, and review criteria behind slides before anyone starts decorating rectangles. Good visual planning turns a presentation from a pile of pretty cards into a guided decision path.

Story before slides Visual intent before art Evidence before confidence Human review before delivery

The slide deck is not the first artifact. The visual brief is.

Many weak AI-assisted presentations fail because the request starts with “make slides.” That skips the audience, the decision, the evidence, the emotional arc, the visual system, and the speaker’s actual purpose.

Ask AI to produce a visual brief first. It should define the audience, the message, the claims, the evidence required, the tone, the slide sequence, and the kinds of visuals that would make each idea easier to understand.

Where AI helps most in visual planning

AI is strongest before production: turning scattered notes into structure, testing whether the argument flows, and proposing visuals that match the job of each slide.

A

Audience translation

AI can rewrite the same material for executives, technical reviewers, frontline users, or skeptical approvers. The underlying facts stay fixed, but the route through them changes.

B

Narrative sequencing

It can turn a heap of ideas into a deck arc: why this matters, what changed, what we learned, what options exist, what we recommend, and what happens next.

C

Visual alternatives

For each idea, AI can suggest diagrams, comparison tables, timelines, decision trees, scorecards, maps, or annotation-heavy screenshots instead of defaulting to bullet confetti.

D

Review scaffolds

AI can create checklists for evidence, accessibility, readability, slide density, source attribution, and whether every visual supports a specific message.

A reliable storyboard flow

Before opening PowerPoint, Keynote, Slides, Canva, or a design tool, build a storyboard that shows the purpose of each slide. This is where AI can be a very useful planning fox.

Set the frame

Define the audience, meeting context, decision needed, and what the deck is not trying to cover.

Name the problem

State the pain, risk, opportunity, or confusion in terms the audience already understands.

Show the evidence

Use data, examples, screenshots, quotes, or workflow evidence. Avoid decorative proof-shaped objects.

Explain the choice

Compare options, tradeoffs, costs, risks, and constraints. Make the decision path visible.

Land the action

End with recommendation, owner, timing, open questions, and the next review gate.

Design decisions AI should not guess silently

Visual planning carries hidden assumptions. Make the model expose them before they become polished nonsense wearing a nice gradient jacket.

!

Data meaning

AI can suggest a chart type, but it should not invent the dataset, the units, the date range, the denominator, or the conclusion. Real numbers need real provenance.

Aa

Accessibility

Ask for large text, high contrast, minimal tiny labels, alt text concepts, and speaker notes that do not depend on color alone. A beautiful unreadable slide is just expensive fog.

✓

Approval path

AI can draft a slide plan, but sensitive claims, legal wording, policy statements, financial data, and customer commitments still need human approval.

Reusable prompt

Use this when you have notes, a draft, or a rough idea and want a visual plan before creating the actual deck.

Act as a slide strategist and visual planning partner.

Goal: Turn the material below into a slide and visual plan, not finished slide copy yet.
Audience: [describe audience]
Presentation purpose: [inform, persuade, train, decide, review, approve]
Decision or action needed: [what should happen after the deck]
Tone: [executive, technical, beginner-friendly, urgent, calm, practical]
Constraints: [brand, length, accessibility, required sources, sensitive topics]

Material:
[paste notes, outline, meeting notes, data, or draft]

Create:
1. A one-sentence message spine for the whole deck.
2. A slide-by-slide storyboard with title, purpose, key claim, evidence needed, recommended visual type, and speaker note guidance.
3. A list of claims that require verification or citation.
4. Visual risks, including clutter, misleading charts, inaccessible color use, unsupported claims, and overconfident wording.
5. A review checklist for human approval before production.

Rules:
- Do not invent data, sources, dates, or approvals.
- Mark uncertain items as assumptions or open questions.
- Prefer diagrams, comparisons, examples, and annotated visuals over generic bullet lists when they help comprehension.

Field guide takeaway: AI is useful for slide and visual planning when it acts like a storyboard partner, not a decoration cannon. Give it the audience, decision, claims, evidence, constraints, and review gates before asking for slides. The result should make the message easier to understand, easier to verify, and easier for a human owner to approve.
