What the summary must preserve

An executive summary is not a tiny version of the whole document. It is a decision lens. The goal is to surface what matters, what is uncertain, what must be decided, and what happens next.

1

Decision needed

Name the decision, approval, prioritization, escalation, or alignment the reader is being asked to make.

2

Current situation

Condense the background into the few facts that change the decision. Do not bury the reader in historical gravel.

3

Options and tradeoffs

Compare plausible paths, including what each option gains, risks, delays, costs, or leaves unresolved.

4

Recommendation

State the preferred path and why it is better than the alternatives. A summary without a point of view can become fog with bullets.

5

Risks

Highlight practical, operational, financial, legal, security, reputation, timing, and adoption risks before they hatch under the rug.

6

Owners

Identify who owns the decision, the work, the review, the communication, and any escalation or approval path.

7

Open questions

Separate unknowns from conclusions. Missing information should be visible, not quietly replaced with confident invention.

8

Next actions

Turn the summary into movement: next steps, dates, owners, dependencies, review gates, and what must happen before commitment.

The practical rule

Ask AI to compress information, not responsibility. The assistant can organize the decision picture, but a human still verifies the facts, owns the recommendation, and approves the message before it travels.

A useful executive summary says: what matters, what we recommend, what can go wrong, and who does what next.

Copy-ready prompt

Paste approved source material only. Replace bracketed text, remove sections that do not apply, and keep the review checks when the summary will influence a real decision.

Decision scaffold 
Role:
Act as a careful executive briefing assistant.  Your job is to turn raw detail into a decision-ready summary without inventing facts, commitments, owners, dates, costs, or conclusions.

Goal:
Create an executive summary for [audience] so they can [decide / approve / prioritize / escalate / understand tradeoffs / prepare for discussion].

Context:
Audience: [executives, directors, project sponsors, committee, technical leadership, etc.]
Topic: [what this is about]
Decision or discussion needed: [specific decision or meeting purpose]
Source material: [paste approved notes, draft, report, transcript, issue list, or project detail]
Known facts that must remain true:
- [fact]
- [fact]
- [fact]

Constraints:
- Do not invent facts, names, dates, numbers, commitments, owners, costs, or approvals.
- Do not make unsupported claims.
- Preserve uncertainty and label assumptions clearly.
- Separate facts from recommendations.
- Keep the tone [clear, neutral, direct, executive, cautious, urgent, etc.].
- Keep the summary to [length or format limit].
- If source material is missing something important, list it under "Open Questions" instead of guessing.

Output format:
Return the summary with these sections:
1. Executive takeaway: 3 to 5 bullets on what matters most.
2. Decision needed: what the reader is being asked to decide, approve, or discuss.
3. Recommendation: the suggested path and the reason for it.
4. Options and tradeoffs: a compact table comparing realistic paths.
5. Key risks: risk, impact, likelihood if known, mitigation, and owner if known.
6. Owners and responsibilities: who owns what, or "not specified" where unclear.
7. Open questions: missing information that could change the recommendation.
8. Next actions: action, owner, due date or timing, dependency, and review gate.

Review criteria:
Before finalizing, check whether the summary:
- Preserves the source facts accurately
- Makes assumptions visible
- Does not overstate certainty
- Clearly distinguishes recommendation from evidence
- Names practical risks and owners
- Gives next actions that someone can actually review

How to feed the summary engine

The quality of the summary depends on the ingredients. Raw detail can be messy, but the prompt should tell the assistant what kind of decision kitchen it is standing in.

Tell it the audience A finance sponsor, security lead, project owner, and senior executive will not need the same framing. Name who is reading.
Name the decision Summaries drift when the decision is absent. Say whether the reader must approve, compare, fund, delay, escalate, or unblock.
Provide source boundaries Tell the AI whether to use only pasted material, approved links, meeting notes, known facts, or a specific document set.
Separate evidence from opinion Ask for facts, recommendation, assumptions, and risks in different sections. Mixed soup makes false confidence harder to spot.
Ask for open questions Unknowns are not failures. Hidden unknowns are the little trapdoors under executive decisions.
Force next actions A summary should end with movement: owners, timing, dependencies, review gates, and what must happen before commitment.

Review before it becomes the meeting memory

Executive summaries have gravity. Once sent, they can become the remembered version of events. Review them carefully before they start wearing a tiny crown.

Facts Check names, dates, numbers, decisions, source claims, dependencies, budgets, and anything that appears unusually precise.
Recommendation Confirm that the recommendation follows from the evidence and does not smuggle in unstated priorities.
Risk Look for missing downsides, weak mitigations, unclear severity, absent owners, and risks softened into polite mist.
Actionability Every next action should have an owner, timing, dependency, or review gate. Otherwise it is just a decorative verb.

Customization matrix

Situation Add this to the prompt Review especially for
Project status summary. Milestones, blockers, timeline changes, owner map, dependency list, and approval needs. False green status, vague blockers, missing owners, and unrealistic next dates.
Decision memo summary. Decision to make, options considered, evaluation criteria, recommendation, and consequences of delay. Unsupported recommendation, one-sided tradeoffs, and hidden assumptions.
Incident or risk briefing. Impact, scope, timeline, containment, customer effect, communications, owner, and escalation path. Understated severity, missing evidence, premature certainty, and unclear accountability.
Budget or investment request. Expected value, cost range, alternatives, opportunity cost, support burden, and measurement plan. Vague ROI, missing support cost, unfunded dependencies, and optimistic timelines.
Policy or governance update. What changes, who is affected, why it matters, compliance requirements, exceptions, and escalation. Invented policy language, unclear scope, missing exception process, and communication gaps.
Technical topic for non-technical leaders. Plain-language framing, business impact, decision points, risks, dependencies, and confidence level. Oversimplification, missing technical constraints, false certainty, and buried operational risk.
Prompt-library takeaway: An executive summary should not merely shorten raw detail. It should turn the detail into a reviewable decision surface: takeaway, recommendation, tradeoffs, risks, owners, open questions, and next actions.
