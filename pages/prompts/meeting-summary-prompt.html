Meeting Summary Prompt

A reusable prompt for turning notes, transcripts, or messy call scribbles into a meeting summary that preserves decisions, owners, due dates, open questions, and follow-ups without inventing certainty. The goal is not to make the meeting sound cleaner than it was. The goal is to make the truth reviewable.

Decisions and non-decisions Owners and due dates Questions and follow-ups

What meeting notes must preserve

A meeting summary becomes shared memory. If it invents certainty, changes who owns what, or turns a discussion into a decision, the tiny note goblin has quietly rewritten reality.

1

Meeting purpose

State why the meeting happened, what topic was covered, and whether the meeting was for alignment, decision, planning, review, or escalation.

2

Decisions made

Capture confirmed decisions only. If people discussed an option but did not approve it, label it as discussion, not decision.

3

Action items

List each follow-up with an owner, due date or timing, dependency, and success condition. Missing fields should stay visible.

4

Owners

Preserve who agreed to do what. Do not assign work to the loudest attendee, the most senior attendee, or the person AI thinks looks plausible.

5

Due dates

Keep dates exact when stated. Use “not specified” when absent. Do not convert vague hopes into calendar commitments.

6

Open questions

Separate unresolved questions, missing data, pending approvals, and assumptions that need confirmation before work continues.

7

Risks and blockers

Preserve concerns, dependencies, conflicts, constraints, and risks raised in the meeting, especially if they affect delivery or approval.

8

Confidence level

Mark weak audio, unclear attribution, conflicting notes, missing attendees, or uncertain phrasing so reviewers know where to look first.

The practical rule

Ask AI to organize the meeting record, not rewrite the meeting. A useful summary separates confirmed decisions from discussion, names owners only when supported, and keeps missing details visible instead of stuffing them under the rug with a confident little flourish.

If the meeting did not decide it, the summary must not pretend it did.

Copy-ready prompt

Paste approved notes, transcript excerpts, or meeting artifacts only. Replace bracketed text and keep the review criteria when the summary will be sent to others.

Meeting scaffold 
Role:
Act as a careful meeting summary assistant.  Your job is to organize the meeting record without inventing decisions, owners, dates, commitments, consensus, approvals, or certainty.

Goal:
Create a clear meeting summary for [audience] so they can review what happened, what was decided, what remains open, and what must happen next.

Context:
Meeting title: [title]
Meeting date and timezone: [date / timezone]
Meeting type: [team sync / project review / incident review / vendor call / leadership meeting / 1:1 / workshop / other]
Audience for the summary: [attendees, leadership, project team, customers, committee, etc.]
Source material: [paste approved notes, transcript, agenda, chat, whiteboard notes, or action list]
Known attendees, if relevant:
- [name / role]
- [name / role]

Constraints:
- Use only the provided meeting material.
- Do not invent decisions, owners, due dates, commitments, approvals, consensus, or next steps.
- If something is implied but not explicit, label it as an assumption or possible interpretation.
- If an owner, due date, dependency, or decision status is unclear, write "Not specified" or "Needs confirmation."
- Separate confirmed decisions from discussion points and proposed ideas.
- Preserve uncertainty, disagreements, blockers, risks, and open questions.
- Keep the tone [clear, neutral, professional, concise, action-oriented, etc.].
- Do not include sensitive side comments, personal remarks, or irrelevant chatter unless they are necessary to the work.

Output format:
Return the meeting summary with these sections:
1. Meeting snapshot: title, date, purpose, and summary in 2 to 4 bullets.
2. Confirmed decisions: decision, context, owner if known, and decision date if known.
3. Action items: action, owner, due date or timing, dependency, and status.
4. Open questions: question, who needs to answer it, and why it matters.
5. Risks, blockers, and concerns: issue, impact, owner if known, and next step if known.
6. Discussion notes: important points that were discussed but not decided.
7. Follow-up messages needed: who should be contacted, why, and what needs confirmation.
8. Items needing human review: unclear attribution, missing owners, vague dates, contradictions, or uncertain conclusions.

Review criteria:
Before finalizing, check whether the summary:
- Clearly separates decisions from discussion
- Avoids invented owners, dates, and commitments
- Makes uncertainty visible
- Preserves open questions and blockers
- Gives follow-ups that can be reviewed by a person
- Does not treat silence as agreement

How to feed the meeting engine

Meeting material is often a nest of timestamps, half-sentences, chat crumbs, side trails, and heroic acronyms. The prompt works best when the assistant knows what kind of memory it is being asked to build.

Name the meeting type A weekly sync, steering meeting, incident review, vendor call, and workshop need different levels of detail and different review pressure.
Include the agenda The agenda helps the assistant sort important discussion from wandering context and identify topics that were skipped or unresolved.
Provide attendee context Names and roles help with ownership, but only if the notes support it. Role context should not become invented accountability.
Flag decision rules Tell the AI whether decisions require approval, consensus, a named approver, a ticket, or follow-up confirmation.
Ask for missing fields Missing owner, missing date, missing approval, and unclear dependency should be marked. Blank spaces are better than fake bricks.
Review before sharing Meeting summaries can become the official record. Check attribution, dates, decisions, sensitive details, and anything that sounds too neat.

Review before it becomes the record

Meeting summaries have sneaky authority. They can turn “we talked about it” into “we agreed to it” unless a human reviewer keeps the ledger clean.

Decisions Confirm each decision was actually made, not merely proposed, discussed, parked, or nodded at by three tired faces on a video call.
Owners and dates Check action owners, due dates, dependencies, and status labels. “Soon” is not a date, and “team” is rarely an owner.
Uncertainty Look for vague attribution, conflicting notes, unclear approvals, weak audio, missing context, and anything the summary made too tidy.
Shareability Remove irrelevant side comments, sensitive details, personal remarks, and internal-only material before the summary leaves the room.

Customization matrix

Meeting type Add this to the prompt Review especially for
Weekly team sync. Current priorities, blockers, owner list, date range, and whether the summary should be brief or action-heavy. Vague owners, recycled blockers, missing dependencies, and action items that quietly survive forever.
Project steering meeting. Decision points, sponsor concerns, escalation needs, milestone changes, budget or scope impacts, and approval rules. Invented approvals, softened risks, unclear decision authority, and hidden timeline changes.
Incident or outage review. Timeline, impact, containment, customer effect, owners, corrective actions, communications, and follow-up review date. Premature root cause, missing evidence, understated impact, and action items without accountable owners.
Vendor or customer call. External attendees, commitments made, commercial or support implications, follow-up messages, and approved sharing boundaries. Accidental commitments, sensitive internal notes, unclear ownership, and facts that require confirmation before sending.
Workshop or brainstorming session. Ideas generated, themes, evaluation criteria, parked questions, next validation steps, and what has not been approved. Ideas incorrectly promoted to decisions, lost minority concerns, and missing validation steps.
One-on-one or performance discussion. Privacy boundaries, agreed actions, support needed, dates, and whether the summary is personal notes or shareable record. Sensitive content, tone, implied commitments, attribution, and anything that should not be distributed.
Prompt-library takeaway: A meeting summary should preserve what happened, not upgrade the meeting into a tidier alternate universe. Keep decisions, owners, due dates, open questions, and follow-ups explicit, and make uncertainty easy to review.
