Prompting Basics Template

A reusable scaffolding for turning a fuzzy request into something AI can answer clearly and something humans can actually review. Use it when the goal matters, the output will be shared, or the first draft needs to land closer to useful than confetti.

The seven-part scaffold

Good prompting is not about secret incantations. It is about giving the assistant enough target, boundary, and review information that the answer can be judged instead of admired from a distance.

1

Role

Tell the AI what perspective to use: editor, analyst, trainer, reviewer, architect, facilitator, or skeptical second reader.

2

Goal

State the job to be done in plain words. A clear goal prevents the answer from wandering into decorative usefulness.

3

Context

Provide the audience, situation, source material, decision background, current draft, known facts, and why the task matters.

4

Constraints

Name the fences: length, tone, policy, tools, jurisdiction, approved sources, privacy limits, budget, deadline, and non-goals.

5

Examples

Show a sample, preferred style, unacceptable pattern, prior version, or tiny before-and-after. Examples are steering wheels.

6

Output format

Ask for bullets, table, email draft, checklist, JSON, markdown, decision memo, risk register, or a specific section structure.

7

Review criteria

Tell the AI how the output will be judged: accuracy, completeness, clarity, risk, citations, assumptions, and actionability.

!

Safety note

Do not paste secrets, credentials, private customer data, restricted material, or internal data that belongs in an approved path only.

The practical rule

The more the output will influence real work, the more the prompt should name the target, the boundaries, and the review standard. Vague prompts create confident fog. Structured prompts create inspectable output.

Do not ask AI to “make it better.” Tell it what better means.

How to use it without overfeeding the context beast

A strong prompt does not need every detail in the universe. It needs the details that change the answer, the constraints that prevent bad output, and the criteria that make review possible.

Start with the decision Before prompting, name what the output is for: inform, decide, draft, compare, summarize, review, plan, or validate.
Separate facts from instructions Put source material under context and instructions under constraints. Mixed soup is how stale assumptions learn to swim.
Use placeholders safely Replace sensitive names, account numbers, emails, customer identifiers, internal URLs, and secrets with labeled placeholders.
Give examples sparingly One good example often beats a long museum of samples. Use examples to show taste, structure, or unacceptable patterns.
Ask for uncertainty Require assumptions, missing context, and confidence warnings when facts, dates, names, numbers, or policies matter.
Make output reviewable Tables, checklists, headings, and explicit risks are easier to inspect than one elegant paragraph wearing a cape.

Stronger prompt

This version gives the AI a job, a boundary, and a review target. The result is easier to evaluate and less likely to become decorative nonsense.

Act as a careful editor for an internal operations audience.

Goal: Improve the clarity of this announcement without changing the meaning.

Context: This message will be sent to employees after a workflow change.  The audience needs to know what is changing, when it starts, and who to contact.

Constraints:
- Preserve all dates, names, team names, and commitments exactly as written.
- Do not add new policy details.
- Keep the tone professional, calm, and direct.
- Keep it under 250 words.
- If something is unclear, list it under "Questions" instead of guessing.

Output format:
1. Revised announcement
2. What changed and why
3. Questions or missing information

Review before you trust the answer

A good prompt improves the odds. It does not transfer accountability. Before using the output, check the parts that can hurt people, money, systems, reputation, or decisions.

Facts Verify names, dates, numbers, policies, source claims, and anything that sounds oddly precise.
Assumptions Look for places where the answer filled gaps without saying so. Silent guesses are slippery little eels.
Constraints Check that tone, format, length, source limits, and non-goals were actually respected.
Risk Review privacy, security, legal, financial, operational, and customer-impact concerns before sending or acting.

Quick customization matrix

Task type Add this to the prompt Review for
Email or communication draft. Audience, relationship, desired tone, facts to preserve, and commitments not to invent. Accuracy, tone, accidental promises, missing context, and whether a human should send it.
Summary. Source material, summary purpose, decision audience, maximum length, and required sections. Omitted decisions, invented conclusions, wrong owner names, and unsupported certainty.
Brainstorming. Problem, audience, constraints, examples of what is too boring or too risky, and ranking criteria. Feasibility, originality, risk, usefulness, and whether ideas need research before adoption.
Review or critique. Review lens, severity levels, what not to rewrite, and whether to provide fixes or just findings. Actionable feedback, false positives, missed risks, and whether the critique matches the goal.
Planning. Scope, non-goals, constraints, dependencies, owners, validation, rollout, rollback, and stop points. Missing prerequisites, fantasy timelines, unclear ownership, unsafe assumptions, and absent validation.
Technical help. Environment, versions, error messages, files involved, commands already run, and what must not change. Wrong assumptions, unsafe commands, unrelated refactors, insufficient validation, and security exposure.
Prompt-library takeaway: The template is not a ritual. It is a workbench. Put the goal, context, constraints, examples, output format, and review criteria on the bench before the AI starts handing you shiny tools.
